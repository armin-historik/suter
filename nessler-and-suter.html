Nessler & Suter

Possible early connections and documented commercial rivalry

OverviewBiographyEugène MethodPatentsCompanyNessler & SuterDocumentsSources

The relationship between Charles Nessler and Eugène Suter deserves its own investigation. It is also a case study in the difference between a memorable historical anecdote and evidence preserved in contemporary legal and technical records.

The reported employment relationship

In her article for the Horgner Jahrheft (2000), Hannelore Kyburz reports that Suter was Nessler’s first employee in London. She attributes the story to the French book Coiffeur – La vie d’un métier by Patrick Amiot. The relevant pages of that book have not yet been examined in this research project.

Kyburz further describes an unspecified “malheur” affecting Nessler, after which Suter supposedly took over the business. The phrase is too vague to support a firm conclusion about a transfer of ownership, premises, equipment or customers. Contemporary business directories, advertisements and legal records would be needed.

Different careers, overlapping markets

Nessler’s pioneering public demonstration in London in 1906 and his subsequent work established an important earlier technical and commercial context. Suter’s later Eugène business developed its own equipment and international distribution. Their histories overlap, but neither can be accurately represented as a simple replacement for the other.

The patent dispute

A reported 1932 United States appellate decision, Nestlé-LeMur Co. v. Eugene Ltd., is a priority primary source for the relationship between the businesses. It concerns patent rights and therefore offers a firmer basis for reconstructing commercial conflict than retrospective stories alone. The decision and the associated patent specifications must be read carefully before stating who prevailed on which legal issue.

Consult the reported appellate decision.

Questions still open

  • Can contemporary London directories place Suter in Nessler’s employment?
  • What precisely does Amiot’s French account say, and what source does it cite?
  • Did any business, lease or equipment actually pass from Nessler to Suter?
  • Which patents and products were at issue in the later litigation?
An alleged personal connection and a documented dispute between later companies are not interchangeable evidence. This page preserves that distinction.
